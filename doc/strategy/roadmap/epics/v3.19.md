# V3.19: The Tickoni Terminal — Visible Discipline

**Labels:** `type/epic`, `area/trust`, `area/audit`, `area/investing`, `area/platform`, `type/enhancement`
**GitHub Issue:** [15](https://github.com/deeprnd/tickoni/issues/15)

## Why This Exists

Institutional-grade financial discipline — the kind that checks policy before acting, records everything irreversibly, and replays decisions exactly — has always lived behind an enterprise gate. It lives in JSONL files. In audit logs. In diagnostic output that only engineers understand. Individual operators, researchers, and small teams who should have access to this level of financial accountability are locked out. Not by ability. By presentation.

V3.19 opens the gate.

Before this increment, Tickoni proved deterministic investment flows, governed runtime boundaries, portfolio consequence, audit, replay, and cross-platform build support. Those were the plumbing. V3.19 turns plumbing into a product: the first surface an operator opens every morning and never puts down.

The terminal doesn't add financial correctness. The runtime already has it. The terminal makes that correctness *visible* — not as a dashboard that pretends to be serious, but as a native desktop terminal built from the same discipline that the runtime enforces: keyboard-driven, high-density, function-first, state-transparent.

## The Product Line

Tickoni turns investment intent into policy-checked, replayable financial proposals — and makes every step visible, verifyable, and reproducible before any action proceeds.

The obvious competitor position for Tickoni is "another investment tool." That frame is wrong and it's the wrong fight. Tickoni doesn't compete on PnL, price movement, or trading speed. Those are the *other* category. Tickoni competes in the category of *verified financial consequence* — the same category as Bloomberg Terminal's compliance mode, but accessible without an enterprise contract, a data center, or a procurement cycle.

The comparison anchor decides how the product is judged. Anchor it against trading platforms and Tickoni loses (it isn't a trading platform). Anchor it against audit tools and Tickoni loses (it's more than audit). Anchor it against institutional financial discipline — the discipline that says "I want to know exactly what happened, why, and whether it can be proven" — and the competition disappears because nobody else is building here.

## Why The Terminal, Why Native, Why Now

### The perception problem

Engineers know Tickoni's trust model works. The runtime checks policy, records audit hashes, replays deterministically, and gates execution. But the operator doesn't see this. The operator sees JSONL files and command-line diagnostics. Perception is cheaper to change than reality, and the reality is already correct. The terminal changes perception at near-zero cost by making the invisible visible.

### The costly signal

A native desktop terminal *costs* something. Building a Qt application that ships on Linux, macOS, and Windows with a disciplined visual system is expensive and slow. That's the point. A flying-by-night operator doesn't build a native terminal. It signals commitment — the same way a company runs an expensive ad campaign and the customers don't even need to watch the ad, they just need to know it exists. The terminal itself is the signal. The trust it makes visible is the substance.

### The infinite-game move

Competitive products chase features: faster charts, more indicators, brighter dashboards. That's finite-game thinking — you win by being faster or flashier than the other guy. Infinite-game thinking asks: "What system do I want to perpetuate?" Tickoni's system is one where financial decisions are *verified before executed*, where audit trails are append-only and hash-chained, where replay proves determinism, where human approval gates money-adjacent actions. The terminal perpetuates that system by making it the daily interface, not the monthly compliance report.

### The Circle of Safety

Leaders create a Circle of Safety inside which people can take risks, communicate honestly, and focus outward at the market. In financial systems, the Circle of Safety is the audit-and-policy layer — the thing that says "if you operate inside these bounds, you are protected." The terminal makes that Circle visible. Every policy check, every hash, every replay result is displayed plainly. The operator *knows* they're inside the Circle. That knowledge changes behavior — not by adding rules, but by making the existing ones perceptible.

## The Demo Moment

This is the moment that answers every skeptical stakeholder in one screen.

The operator opens the Tickoni terminal — native, desktop, no browser — and sees the AI-infrastructure case:

- The original thesis and normalized mandate. Plain text. No reverse-engineering required.
- Account cash and buying power. Current, fresh, with a freshness timestamp.
- An allowed basket and paper ticket. Structured. Verifiable.
- Restricted `SOXL` in a rejected-instruments table. Exact denial reason. No hidden affordances for what was blocked.
- Every policy check that ran. What it measured. The limit it compared against. The outcome.
- Before/after cash, buying power, and exposure. The consequence, calculated and displayed.
- Proposal, policy, and event hashes. Chainable. Verifiable.
- Replay MATCH and tamper CLEAN. The decision reproduced exactly from captured inputs.
- A governed `PLACE PAPER` or `SAVE` action that's enabled only when an existing runtime contract permits it.

Then the operator switches to the oversized fixture on the same screen. No page reload. No raw log dump. The terminal shows the blocking limit, the exact reason, and a disabled action. The skeptical stakeholder now understands: this system says *no* visibly, consistently, and without exception.

That demo replaces reading fixture output, auditing JSONL, and explaining the runtime over three Slack threads.

## Strategic Positioning

### What we're not (and why that matters)

This is not a trading platform. It will never be a trading platform. Trading platforms optimize for speed of action. Tickoni optimizes for *verified consequence before action*. These are fundamentally different design objectives. A trading platform that adds audit is audit as an afterthought. Tickoni's terminal is audit as the interface.

This is not a browser dashboard. Browser dashboards have become the visual equivalent of beige — they work, but they signal "generic tool built by committee." Professional financial operators expect a native terminal. The comparison effect means a browser-based terminal feels cheap against every real terminal on the market. A native terminal doesn't need to be better than Bloomberg to be credible here; it needs to *look* like it belongs in that category.

This is not gamified investing. No hero cards. No leaderboards. No dopamine-style celebration. Those features target amateur behavior. Tickoni targets disciplined operators who want verified financial consequence, not engagement metrics.

### The market opportunity

Financial discipline tools are expensive because they're built for enterprises with thousands of employees and procurement departments. No one has built one that's *designed* to be accessible to individual operators from day one. The terminal is that product. It's not a watered-down enterprise tool — it's a full-featured terminal built for the constraint of accessibility, which makes it sharper than both enterprise tools (which optimize for feature count) and retail tools (which optimize for engagement).

### The reframing

The existing product line says: "Tickoni turns investment intent into policy-checked, replayable financial proposals." That's accurate. It's also written by an engineer, for an engineer, in the language of system behavior.

The market-facing frame is: "Institutional financial discipline, visible to everyone." Same system. Different category.

Think of it like the Diamond Shreddies example: same cereal, different shape, different sales. The product hasn't changed meaningfully — the *meaning* has. The terminal is Tickoni's Diamond Shreddies moment. It's the same system, presented in the category where it actually wins.

## Users And Jobs

- Investment user: enter or inspect an investment thesis and understand the bounded proposal before any action. The job is "I need to know what I'm about to do and whether it's actually allowed."
- CaseOps operator: move quickly between investment cases, policy reasons, consequence, and proof. The job is "I need to process these cases without reading raw logs."
- Compliance or risk reviewer: inspect exact policy gates, hashes, evidence references, and replay outcome. The job is "I need to verify this, not trust a summary."
- Product reviewer: run deterministic allowed, denied, oversized, replay, and tamper scenarios from one native application. The job is "I need to prove the system works, not explain that it does."
- Runtime owner: see a compact terminal/API availability summary sufficient to distinguish unavailable data from a policy denial. The job is "I need to know the system is alive without firing up a diagnostics console."

## Architecture And Ownership

The terminal remains outside the deterministic financial runtime:

```text
+-----------------------------+
| Tickoni Qt Terminal         |
| tk_ui tile sandbox          |
| QML views + C++ models      |
+-------------+---------------+
              |
              | shared-memory command channel
              | shared-memory event/value channels
              v
+-----------------------------+
| tk_api                      |
| Semantic API tile           |
+-------------+---------------+
              |
  +-----------+-----------+----------------+----------------+
  |                       |                |                |
  v                       v                v                v
 tkpoly                  tkaudt           tkrepl           tkmetr/tkdiag
 policy authority        audit truth      replay truth     availability only

Remote path:
  tk_ui -> local bus -> tk_gateway_client -> external protocol
                                       -> remote tk_gateway -> remote tk_api
```

- `tk_ui` is a composite edge tile wrapping the Qt application. `tk_ui` owns the local shared-memory channels, bus bridge, command admission, feature projection stores, presentation models, and collection models.
- `tk_api` is the semantic API tile. It is not defined by HTTP. It owns capability negotiation, subscriptions, request validation, expected-revision validation, governed command routing, query and command response semantics, state revisions and freshness, and event projection contracts.
- A separate `tk_gateway` tile owns external network protocols (HTTP, WebSocket, QUIC) and translates them to typed internal tk_api messages for remote clients.
- `tkpoly` remains the only authority for finance-native policy outcomes.
- `tkaudt` remains the source of material event history and audit identities.
- `tkrepl` remains the source of replay match, divergence, capsule, and tamper state.
- `tkmodl`, `tktool`, and `tkadpt` remain hard boundaries for model, tool, and adapter access.
- Future `tkexec` remains disabled unless a separate approved-execution epic explicitly enables a sandbox or live path.
- The terminal may cache presentation data, but cache contents are never a source of financial truth.
- A shared-memory channel overflow, sequence gap, resynchronization failure, or bus disconnect must transition action state to unavailable or resynchronizing.

## Qt Technical Foundation

Use:

```text
Qt 6
+-- Qt Quick / QML              terminal shell and visual composition
+-- Qt Quick Controls           controls with a custom Tickoni style
+-- C++                         application state, bus bridge, commands
+-- Presentation models         focused view models (CaseViewModel, etc.)
+-- Feature stores              read-optimized projection stores
+-- QAbstractItemModel          basket, policy, impact, evidence tables
+-- Shared-memory channels      tk_api tile link (ui_cmd, ui_rsp, ui_evt, ui_lval)
+-- CMake                       desktop build and packaging integration
```

### Required Separation

- QML owns layout, binding, focus, transitions, and presentation only.
- Presentation models own typed Q_PROPERTY values, local presentation state, command submission through application use cases, and stale/unavailable states.
- Feature stores own validated snapshots, event application, revision tracking, and produce immutable ModelPatches for GUI-thread model updates.
- The bus bridge owns shared-memory workspace attachment, channel cursors, message schema validation, sequence gap detection, process-generation checks, bulk payload resolution, and handoff to stores. It is not exposed to QML.
- Application use cases own command validation, request ID allocation, idempotency, and command enqueueing. They do not contain QML types or shared-memory offsets.
- Collection models use QAbstractItemModel subclasses for large collections. All model mutations occur on the GUI thread.
- QML must not call shared-memory descriptors, network protocols, tile links, model providers, adapters, files, shell commands, or execution systems directly.
- QML must not publish directly to tile links or global event buses. A QML control calls only a typed method on its focused presentation model.
- Financial policy must not be recomputed in QML or C++ terminal code.
- Display models may derive presentation-only values such as formatted text, column widths, and style roles from governed semantic state.
- Runtime semantic values must not be inferred from colors, labels, or icon state.

## Visual Identity: Midnight Oni

The terminal should feel disciplined, technical, and premium rather than colorful or decorative.

The visual concept is:

```text
cold machine surfaces
+ blue Tickoni intelligence
+ restrained amber terminal interaction
+ rare vermilion brand seal
+ strict semantic state colors
```

### Color Responsibilities

Brand color and operational state must remain separate.

| Role | Purpose | Suggested value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon black | Main workspace | `#070A0E` |
| Midnight navy | Standard panel | `#0B1119` |
| Steel navy | Raised or selected surface | `#101923` |
| Dark slate | Subtle divider | `#192431` |
| Blue-gray | Strong divider | `#263445` |
| Cool white | Primary text | `#E7EDF4` |
| Muted steel | Secondary text | `#8D9AAA` |
| Disabled gray | Unavailable text | `#566270` |
| Oni blue | Tickoni identity and active navigation | `#3478D4` |
| Active blue | Focus, selected step, link | `#4A9EFF` |
| Terminal amber | Prompt, function cue, shortcut | `#E8A83E` |
| Wealth vermilion | Rare brand seal or oni detail | `#A9343D` |
| Pass green | Allowed, pass, replay match | `#47A66A` |
| Signal red | Denied, failed, divergence | `#E0525E` |
| Warning amber | Approval, evidence, stale, near limit | `#D69A32` |

### Color Rules

- Blue identifies Tickoni, current focus, selected function, active case, evidence navigation, and the current workflow step.
- Terminal amber identifies the command prompt, function mnemonic, keyboard shortcut, timestamp emphasis, and small operator cues.
- Wealth vermilion is a rare brand element only: oni eye, horn, seal, premium report mark, or equivalent identity detail.
- Wealth vermilion must not mean allowed, profitable, successful, selected, or safe.
- Signal red is reserved for operational denial, failure, tamper, divergence, and destructive confirmation.
- Green is reserved for allowed, pass, healthy, replay match, and clean proof.
- Warning amber is reserved for approval required, evidence missing, stale state, near-limit, or degraded availability.
- Gray means pending, disabled, unavailable, inactive, or not applicable.
- Color is never the only carrier of meaning. Every state also requires text, a code, or a distinct symbol.

### Color Proportion

The default workspace should remain predominantly neutral:

```text
80% black, navy, and gray surfaces
15% white and muted information text
 4% Tickoni blue focus and navigation
 1% amber, vermilion, and semantic state accents combined
```

This is a discipline target rather than a pixel-level test. Large saturated panel fills, rainbow tables, multi-color gradients, glowing borders, and colored drop shadows are prohibited.

### Status Treatment

Prefer:

- a thin colored left rule;
- compact text status;
- a small square or dot;
- one-pixel underline;
- restrained badge outline;
- direct table-cell state.

Avoid large filled status cards.

Example:

```text
| ALLOWED      green rule + green text
| DENIED       signal-red rule + signal-red text
| APPROVAL     warning-amber rule + amber text
| UNAVAILABLE  gray rule + gray text
```

### Typography

- Use a compact sans-serif for labels and navigation.
- Use a monospace or tabular-number face for values, commands, hashes, IDs, and dense tables.
- Align decimals and currency units.
- Use no more than three primary text sizes in the default workspace.
- Use uppercase for short function names and statuses, not for paragraphs.
- Fonts must have redistributable licensing appropriate to the selected Qt distribution model; no font file is committed without license review.

### Borders, Shape, And Motion

- Thin gray-blue dividers.
- Sharp rectangles or minimal radius.
- No consumer-dashboard card shadows.
- Motion is limited to focus transition, command completion, connection state, replay progress, and critical state change.
- No celebratory animation.
- Hidden or background panels must not continuously animate or redraw.

## Information Architecture

Use five top-level functions in V3.19:

```text
CASE
POLICY
IMPACT
PROOF
SYSTEM
```

- `CASE`: default investment workbench.
- `POLICY`: exact checks, limits, observed values, and denied reasons.
- `IMPACT`: cash, buying power, exposure, and concentration before/after.
- `PROOF`: audit identity, evidence, replay, tamper, and divergence summary.
- `SYSTEM`: compact source, freshness, bus status, gateway availability, fixture/runtime mode. It is not a full operations console.

Deferred top-level areas include `PAYMENTS`, `CASEOPS`, `CAPABILITIES`, `ADAPTERS`, `HEALTH`, and a full `AUDIT` explorer.

## Story Breakdown

Six stories implement the V3.19 terminal, each mapping to one of the five information-architecture functions plus one documentation/closure story:

- **V3.19.S1 — Terminal Shell, Navigation, And Command Bar**
  Terminal frame, Midnight Oni visual system, keyboard navigation, command bar, five-function navigation bar (CASE, POLICY, IMPACT, PROOF, SYSTEM), status strip, responsive panel grid. Story 1.

- **V3.19.S2 — Investment Safe-Action Workbench (CASE Function)**
  Normalized intent, allowed basket, rejected candidates, ticket preview, policy check display, proposal hash, paper state indicator, restricted instrument handling (SOXL scenario), oversize notional blocking (USD 25,000 scenario). Story 2.

- **V3.19.S3 — removed.** Payment guard workspace is out of scope: the epic explicitly excludes payment, payout, retry, transfer, or beneficiary workflows. Story removed.

- **V3.19.S4 — Before/After Impact View (IMPACT Function)**
  Cash, buying power, sector exposure, ETF exposure, single-name concentration, compact alerts. Investment-only impact (no combined payment obligation workspace). Story 4.

- **V3.19.S5 — Proof Black Box (PROOF Function)**
  9-step audit timeline, hash fields, evidence refs, model route, adapter attribution, approval state, replay status (MATCH/DIVERGE/MISSING), tamper check (CLEAN/TAMPERED), proof export, divergence details. Story 5.

- **V3.19.S6 — System Availability Panel (SYSTEM Function)**
  Compact data freshness, local bus status (connected, resynchronizing, overflow), sequence gap detection, process generation validity, gateway availability (remote mode), fixture/runtime mode indicator. Not a full operations console. Story 6.

- **V3.19.S7 — API Contracts, Fixtures, Screenshots, And Release Evidence**
  `InvestmentCaseView` aggregated read contract for `tk_api` over shared-memory channels, fixture file paths and schemas for all five functions, bus-transport test contract (channel overflow, sequence gaps, resynchronization), screenshot checklist, blocked-flow examples, documentation that `tk_ui` communicates only through `tk_api` (shared-memory for local, `tk_gateway` for remote). Story 7.
